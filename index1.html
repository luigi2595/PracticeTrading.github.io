ES Practice Lab

A flight simulator for E-mini S&P 500 futures. You practise the parts that are actually trainable — reading a break, placing risk, sitting still — without money on the line.

  • 1Pattern lab — start here. You get a chart stopped at a decision: a level just broke. Was that real selling, or stops being run? Call it, place your stop, and watch what happened next. Turn on Hard mode once the tells start making sense.
  • 2Replay sim — a full session plays out bar by bar. Buy and sell from the bar at the bottom. Every trade carries real commissions and slippage, so the P&L is honest. Set a daily loss cap and the app will refuse the trade that would blow through it.
  • 3Journal — every trade logs itself. Win rate, expectancy in R, how far trades went against you before they worked, and which setup tag is quietly paying for all the others.

The price data is synthetic — built to move like an intraday session, but it is not the real ES and contains no real news. Treat this as practice for execution and risk habits, not as a place to discover a strategy. Nothing here is trading advice, and futures can lose more than you deposit. Your journal is saved on your own device and is not shared with anyone.

How to read these numbers
R is the trade's result divided by the money you had at risk when you entered (stop distance × $50 × contracts). Thinking in R instead of dollars is the single fastest way to stop sizing emotionally.
Expectancy = average R per trade. Positive means the edge survives your losers. Anything above +0.15R with 50+ trades is a real, tradeable edge.
Profit factor = gross wins ÷ gross losses. Below 1.0 you are paying the market to practise; 1.3–1.6 is a solid intraday number.
MAE (max adverse excursion) = how far the trade went against you before it worked. If your winners rarely go more than 3 points against you, your stop is probably wider than it needs to be.
MFE (max favourable excursion) = how far it went your way. Winners with big MFE and small capture mean you're exiting early.

Anatomy of a failed breakdown (and how it differs from a real one)
A failed breakdown is not a pattern you see — it is a sequence you wait for. Price grinds along an obvious low that everyone can see. Resting sell-stops pile up under it. Something pushes price just far enough through to trigger them, those stops become market sell orders, and the traders who wanted to be long down there get filled by that forced selling. Then the selling is finished — because it was never real selling, it was liquidation — and price snaps back into the range. The snap-back is fast precisely because nobody is left to sell.

The four things that separate a stop run from a real break, all visible at the moment of decision:
1. Extension. How far past the level did it travel, measured in ATRs, not points? A stop run rarely needs more than about one ATR. Real repricing runs further because new sellers keep hitting bids.
2. Closes. Wicks below a level are noise; closes below it are agreement. One or two closes beyond = still a run. Four, five, six consecutive closes beyond = the market has accepted the new price.
3. Volume. A break on ordinary volume is nobody's idea — it is stops. A break on 2× volume that keeps trading heavy is participation.
4. Time. Stop runs are quick. The longer price is content to sit beyond the level without bouncing, the more it is telling you it belongs there.

The trigger matters more than the read. Being right that a breakdown failed is worth nothing if you buy while it is still falling. The entry is the reclaim — the first close back inside the level — and your risk is the low of the sweep. If that low breaks again, you were wrong, cheaply. Traders lose money on this pattern not by misreading it but by front-running it.

Reversals follow the same logic one level up. An exhaustion reversal needs a stretched, tiring trend (distance from the mean), a climax (range and volume expansion on the final push) and then a trigger (a close back through the climax bar). Two out of three is a losing trade. A trend that is merely "far up" is not a reversal — it is a trend.

Market generator

Export 1-minute bars from TradingView, NinjaTrader, Sierra or anything that writes CSV. It needs date, time, open, high, low, close and ideally volume — column order and header names are worked out for you, and multiple days are split into separate sessions automatically.

Same seed = same session, so you can replay one day repeatedly and test whether a different plan would have worked. Seed is shown on every new session. VWAP is anchored at the 09:30 open (RTH), the way most ES traders run it, and every level is computed only from bars that have already printed — the chart never knows the future.

What this simulator does and does not teach
Synthetic price is built to have the statistical texture of an intraday ES session — volatility clustering, trending and choppy regimes, an active open and close, gaps, and occasional shocks. What it cannot contain is real market context: the actual news, the order flow, the levels other traders are watching. So treat it as a flight simulator for execution and risk habits — order placement, stop discipline, sizing, sitting through noise, not revenge trading — rather than a place to discover a strategy that will then work on the real ES.

The reference levels are real in the sense that they are computed correctly from the session in front of you — VWAP, the opening range, session and overnight extremes. What synthetic data cannot give you is the reason a level matters on a given day: yesterday's rejection, an economic release, a big seller who keeps showing up at one price. So use the sim to build the habit of always knowing where price is relative to VWAP and the opening range, and get the context from real charts.

Practising on synthetic data also can't reproduce the psychology of real money at risk, which is the part that breaks most traders. The usual bridge is: build the habits here → run the same rules on live data in a broker demo → trade one micro (MES, $5/point) with real money → scale only after a written, dated log of consistent results.
